Edu · Three.js

See how the code moves the camera.

A PerspectiveCamera is just six numbers — field of view, aspect, near, far, position, and where it looks. This tool draws all of them at once so you can watch the view frustum open up, intersect the screen, and decide what your canvas actually shows.

The scene setup panel shows the equivalent three.js code; the controls beneath it drive each variable with a slider, a number field, or the ▲▼ buttons, and the model updates live. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom. On a phone the panel sits below the view; on a wide screen it docks to the side.

How to read it

Start from one idea: the screen is the only real thing here. It's the physical canvas — the pixels that actually reach you. Everything else — the camera, its frustum, the teapot, the axes — is a coordinate construct that exists only to explain what arrives on that surface. So the screen stays fixed, and the constructs move within the imagined space around it. This is the browser's own point of view: it never sees a 3D world, only the finished image.

The orange shape is the camera's frustum, its view volume from the near plane out to the far plane. Both the camera and the teapot can be moved with the controls — drag either through the scene and watch the rendered image on the screen update in step. At the default settings the frustum's edges meet the screen's corners, which is what "correctly framed" looks like.

The screen carries two coordinate systems at once. In browser pixels the origin is the top-left corner, X runs right and Y runs down, ending at the renderer.setSize dimensions. In three.js units the origin is the center, X runs right and Y runs up. Reading the same corner in both systems is the whole point: it shows the conversion the renderer does on every frame.

Try pushing fov up and watch the frustum widen; pull the camera's z back and watch the teapot shrink on the screen; slide the teapot left or right and watch its image slide the opposite way; swap the aspect-ratio presets and watch the screen reshape. Use ⊕ frame view to recenter your vantage if a construct wanders out of sight.

The image painted on the screen is the actual frame the camera renders — the teapot is drawn a second time, offscreen, through the simulated camera, and that result is mapped onto the screen. So it isn't an approximation of what the camera sees; it is what the camera sees.

What each number means

renderer.setSize(width, height)
The canvas size in pixels. Only the ratio matters here — it drives the aspect, which sets the screen's shape.
fov
Vertical field of view, in degrees. Larger means a wider frustum and more of the scene squeezed into the frame.
aspect
Width ÷ height. Stretches the frustum horizontally so the image isn't distorted on a non-square canvas.
near / far
The closest and farthest distances the camera renders. Anything nearer than near or beyond far is clipped.
camera.position.set(x, y, z)
Where the camera sits in three.js world units. +z is toward the viewer, so the default (0, 0, 5) sits in front of the screen looking back at it.
camera.lookAt(x, y, z)
The point the camera aims at. The dashed line of sight runs from the camera to this target.
